## 0002.1 — measurement harness

`crates/engine_stress` runs seven deterministic scenarios and records counters
that are identical on every machine. Counters are committed to
`fixtures/stress/baseline.json` and asserted in CI; timings are printed, never
committed, never asserted.

Byte accounting was added to the engine to support it: `Volume::cell_bytes`,
`Volume::palette_bytes`, `MeshData::cpu_bytes`, and mesh bytes/indices on
`CacheStats`.

### Baseline, as first recorded

Measured in release on a 4-core cloud container. Counters are exact and asserted;
timings are indicative only.

| scenario | volumes | cells | faces | quads | merge | cell+palette | mesh | build ms | median edit ms |
|----------|--------:|------:|------:|------:|------:|-------------:|-----:|---------:|---------------:|
| dense_solid | 64 | 262,144 | 24,576 | 96 | 256.0x | 512 KiB | 20 KiB | 54.7 | — |
| sparse_terrain | 111 | 259,044 | 49,448 | 9,504 | 5.2x | 889 KiB | 2.0 MiB | 88.1 | — |
| checker | 8 | 16,384 | 98,304 | 98,304 | 1.0x | 64 KiB | 20.2 MiB | 21.4 | — |
| material_stress | 8 | 32,768 | 6,144 | 6,144 | 1.0x | 66 KiB | 1.3 MiB | 5.4 | — |
| edit_storm | 8 | 32,646 | 6,844 | 674 | 10.2x | 64 KiB | 142 KiB | 4.7 | 0.445 |
| boundary_storm | 8 | 32,644 | 6,708 | 696 | 9.6x | 64 KiB | 147 KiB | 4.6 | 0.932 |
| travel_strip | 110 | 249,780 | 55,666 | 8,856 | 6.3x | 881 KiB | 1.8 MiB | 113.5 | — |

### What the first baseline already settles

**Mesh data dominates, and by how much depends entirely on the shape.** On
realistic terrain mesh is ~2.3x cell storage — close to the 2:1 estimated from
the DROP 0001 demo scene, so that estimate holds up. But the spread is enormous:
a solid world costs *more to store than to draw* (20 KiB of mesh against 512 KiB
of cells), while the checker case inverts it completely at **316x** — 20.2 MiB of
mesh over 64 KiB of cells, or 1,296 bytes of mesh per occupied cell.

This matters for the residency budget in 0002.10: a budget derived from cell
counts would be wrong by two orders of magnitude depending on terrain shape.
**Budget on mesh bytes, measured, not on cell or chunk counts.**

**Merging is not uniformly valuable.** 256x on solid, 5-6x on terrain, and
exactly 1.0x on both checker and material_stress. The material_stress result is
the interesting one: the shape is a plain solid block whose shell is only 6,144
faces, but scattered materials block every merge. Material variety costs as much
as geometric complexity.

**Boundary edits cost about twice an interior edit at the median, and far more at
the tail** — 0.932 ms against 0.445 ms median, with a 5.9 ms worst case against
0.6 ms. That is the neighbour-invalidation rule doing exactly what it should, and
it is a measured argument for the scheduler in 0002.8 needing to coalesce.

**Nothing here is a frame-rate claim.** These are CPU-side compile measurements on
a software-rendering container.

## 0002.2 — spatial-role separation

The five conflated ideas now have separate names and separate types.

| was | is | role |
|-----|----|------|
| `ChunkPos` | `VolumePos` | the 16³ storage leaf |
| — | `RegionPos` | residency and persistence, 8×8×8 volumes |
| — | `RenderSectionId` | a unit of compiled geometry |
| `ChunkMeshCache` | `SectionMeshCache` | keyed by section, not by volume |
| `Chunk` struct | *deleted* | it was a wrapper that **was** a volume |

`SectionGrid` maps volumes onto sections. It is one-to-one today, which is a fine
implementation and a terrible assumption — so every caller goes through it rather
than using a volume address as a section address. `SectionGrid::new(2)` already
works and is tested: eight volumes become one submission, with the world model
untouched.

Grouping **concatenates** quads, it never merges across a volume boundary. The
tests assert that explicitly: a grouped section and eight separate sections
produce the *same quad count* and the *same unit faces*, differing only in how
many submissions the renderer receives. Cross-volume merging would change the
geometry and needs its own correctness story, so it stays out of this drop.

The world now reports dirty **volumes** and the cache maps them to sections,
deduplicating as it goes — four dirty volumes sharing a section cause one rebuild,
not four. That deduplication is what makes a larger section grid affordable later.

### The refactor changed no behaviour, and that is checkable

Every stress counter is byte-identical to the baseline recorded in 0002.1. That is
the harness earning its keep one pass after being built: a restructure this broad
would otherwise have been argued about rather than verified.

115 tests pass, including nine new spatial tests in `engine_core` and four new
section-separation tests in `engine_geometry`.

### Deliberately not done here

`RegionPos` exists as an address; the region *container*, ownership of volumes and
load-order independence are 0002.4. The meshers still compile one volume at a
time — generalising them to arbitrary cell bounds is what cross-volume merging
would need, and it is out of scope.

## 0002.3 — floating render origin

World coordinates stay integer and authoritative; the world never moves. Rendering
converts `global − render origin → small local → f32`, with the origin following
the camera in region-aligned steps.

| piece | where |
|-------|-------|
| `GlobalPos` — authoritative f64 cell-space position | `engine_core::origin` |
| `RenderOrigin` — aligned anchor, rebase with hysteresis | `engine_core::origin` |
| section-local vertex positions | `MeshData::origin` |
| camera position, transform derived | `apps/sandbox/src/camera.rs` |

### Why mesh positions became section-local here

If vertices carried global positions, an origin shift would mean rebuilding every
mesh. Emitting them relative to the owning section's minimum cell means a rebase
costs **one transform update per section and no mesh rebuilds at all**. It also
keeps every vertex coordinate inside a section's own extent, so `f32` precision is
constant no matter how far the player has travelled.

This was listed as a 0002.12 memory candidate. It moved forward because floating
origin needs it structurally, not because it saves bytes.

### Constants

`ORIGIN_ALIGN_CELLS` is one region (128 cells), so an anchor never splits a
residency unit. `ORIGIN_REBASE_DISTANCE_CELLS` is two regions, which gives the
same hysteresis idea the streaming radii will use: hovering on a boundary does not
rebase every frame.

### Verified

Nine unit tests cover alignment below zero, round trips across zero, rebasing
preserving every relative position, edits resolving to the same cell either side
of a shift, and render coordinates staying under 1,000 at four million cells out.

Also verified in the running app: flying ~700 cells moved the camera to
`-358 -225 -420` with the origin following to `-256 -256 -384` — every component a
multiple of 128, and the camera within 102 cells of its anchor, so the hysteresis
held. No visual discontinuity, no crash.

A latent bug went with it: `raycast` previously built its DDA from absolute `f32`
coordinates, which quietly loses sub-cell accuracy far from the origin. It now
takes a `GlobalPos` and converts only the fraction within the start cell.

Stress counters are unchanged again — section-local positions alter vertex values,
not vertex counts.

## 0002.4 — region layer

Storage is now two levels: the world owns `Region`s, a region owns the 16³ volumes
inside it. Loading or evicting a region is one map insert or removal rather than
512 of them, which is the whole reason the tier exists.

`World::insert_region` and `World::remove_region` are the streaming primitives.
Both mark the right things dirty: every volume that arrives or departs, **and**
every volume in a neighbouring region that touched the seam — because faces that
were hidden against a loaded neighbour become exposed when it goes away. That is
the 0002.2 neighbour rule extended across a region boundary, and it is tested in
both directions.

### Two different dirtinesses

They are easy to conflate and expensive to confuse:

| dirty | means | tracked by |
|-------|-------|-----------|
| volume | geometry needs rebuilding | `World::take_dirty` |
| region | has unsaved edits | `Region::is_dirty` |

Losing the second is how a streaming engine silently eats a player's work, so a
region loaded from disk starts clean and any edit marks it unsaved.

### Iteration order, made explicit

Volume iteration is now **region-major**, which is deterministic but not the same
as globally ascending volume order. The save format used to lean on the world's
iteration order being sorted; it now sorts explicitly via `World::volumes_sorted`.
The committed-fixture test proves the output is byte-identical to before.

### A semantic that got clearer

An emptied volume is now indistinguishable from one that never existed —
`volume_count` counts populated volumes only. Its storage genuinely lingers until
compaction, which `allocated_volume_count` reports and `footprint` still charges
for, because that memory really is still allocated.

### Verified

Thirteen region tests: tiling without gaps or overlap across region seams,
negative coordinates, boundary cells, eviction round-tripping exactly, dirty-region
tracking, ascending iteration, residency accounting, and the one that matters most
for streaming — **world contents are identical whatever order regions are loaded
in**, checked forwards, backwards and interleaved.

148 tests pass. The stress baseline moved by exactly one field, `resident_regions`,
from 0 to its real value; every other counter is unchanged, so the restructure
again altered no behaviour. `travel_strip` reports 4 regions for its 32-volume
length, as it should.

## 0002.5 — persistence v2

A sharded directory: `world.json` manifest plus `regions/r.<x>.<y>.<z>.json`. See
[`native-world-format.md`](native-world-format.md) for the schema.

The point is not tidiness. v1 held the whole world in one file, so loading a
single region meant parsing everything and saving one edit meant rewriting
everything. `load_region` and `save_region` now touch exactly one shard — the
streaming primitive — and `save_dirty_regions` writes only regions with unsaved
edits and marks them clean, which is what makes "save before evict" affordable.

The manifest adds what v1 had nowhere to put: world id, name, spawn, and a
placeholder for generator settings. `WorldMeta` is deliberately separate from
`World`, so "do these two worlds hold the same geometry?" stays a question about
geometry.

### Failure modes are loud

A world that silently loses a region is worse than one that refuses to load. Four
distinct errors, each tested: a listed shard missing from disk, a shard whose
`pos` disagrees with the name it was loaded as, a volume that does not belong to
the region claiming it, and a directory with no manifest.

### Verified

Twenty-one v2 tests alongside the twenty v1 tests, which all still pass
unchanged. Coverage: round trips, canonical byte-identical output, edit history
not affecting bytes, single-region load, load-order independence through disk,
dirty-only saves, **edit → save → evict → reload preserving the edit exactly**,
every failure mode above, forward tolerance of unknown keys, v1 migration
including the committed DROP 0001 fixture, and a committed v2 fixture that must
stay canonical.

## 0002.6 — residency state machine

New crate `engine_stream`, which holds streaming policy and does no I/O and knows
nothing about Bevy. It decides *what should happen*; a host carries it out and
reports back. That separation is what makes every awkward case reachable in a
unit test.

`ResidencyTable` tracks each region's lifecycle:

```text
Unloaded → Requested → Loading → Resident → Meshing → Ready
                           ↓         ↑__________________│   (edits re-mesh)
                       Unloaded

Ready/Resident → EvictRequested → Saving (if dirty) → Unloaded
```

Transitions are checked. An illegal one returns an error naming the region and
both states rather than silently corrupting residency.

### Why this is not a pile of booleans

"Is this region loaded?" is several questions that disagree constantly: does a
shard exist, has a load been requested, is one already in flight, are the cells in
memory, are there unsaved edits, is geometry compiled, is it uploaded, is it safe
to throw away. Independent flags produce states that cannot happen — a region
loading twice, a dirty region evicted before its save completed, a mesh uploaded
for cells that are gone.

Three behaviours fall out of making it explicit, and each is tested:

- **A second request never starts a second load.** Duplicate in-flight loads are
  prevented, not discouraged.
- **A dirty region is never evictable.** Dropping one is refused outright; the
  save must finish first. Losing this is how a streaming engine eats a player's
  work.
- **Wanting a region again cancels its eviction**, as does editing it mid-flight.
  Rapid direction changes must not throw away what the camera turned back towards.

### Verified

Eighteen tests: the happy path, duplicate requests, every illegal transition
refused by name, failed loads leaving no trace, edits making geometry stale,
save-before-drop, clean regions skipping the save, eviction cancelled by renewed
interest and by editing, stale mesh results abandoned and rescheduled, a mesh
completing after eviction was scheduled (harmless, no resurrection),
least-recently-needed candidate ordering, and candidates excluding everything
unsafe.

187 tests pass overall.

## 0002.7 — async mesh jobs and the stale-result rule

**A result compiled from older cells must never overwrite newer geometry.** Get
this wrong and the symptom is a carved wall reappearing a few frames later, or a
hole that never fills in — intermittent, timing-dependent, and close to impossible
to reproduce by hand.

So every job records a `SectionFingerprint` of the cells it was built from, and a
returning result is checked against the world as it is *now*. If anything moved,
the result is discarded and the section is requeued. Nothing ever blocks waiting
for a job.

### Neighbours are in the fingerprint, and that is the whole subtlety

A section's geometry does not depend only on its own cells. Cross-volume occlusion
means a face is emitted or hidden according to the cell *across the boundary*,
which belongs to a different volume and possibly a different region. A fingerprint
covering only the section's own volumes would call a result fresh when the
neighbour hiding half its faces had just been carved away.

The fingerprint also distinguishes an **absent** volume from an **untouched**
one — `None` versus `Some(revision)` — because a neighbour appearing or vanishing
changes a section's geometry just as much as one being edited.

### A job owns its cells

`MeshJobInput` snapshots every volume it needs, which makes it `Send` and entirely
independent of the live world: a worker compiles it on any thread with no locking
and no borrow. The cost is a copy of the section's volumes plus their neighbours,
which is small and predictable with jobs bounded. Copying only the boundary shells
would be cheaper and is a later optimisation.

A test spawns a real thread, edits the world while it runs, and confirms the
result comes back and is correctly rejected.

### Deduplication

Editing one section a hundred times before the mesher reaches it produces **one**
pending job at the newest revision, not a hundred rebuilds of stale states. The
queue is a set, so this is structural rather than a policy that can be forgotten.

### Verified

Eighteen tests covering every case the scope lists: edit during a running job,
several edits before the first result, an edit in a *neighbouring* volume, a
neighbour appearing or vanishing, a section cancelled mid-flight, a result
arriving twice, a region evicted mid-job, the same address reloaded with different
contents, jobs completing out of order each judged on their own, bounded work in
flight, compiling on another thread, and a snapshot compiling to geometry
identical to the live world.

205 tests pass.

## 0002.8 — prioritized bounded scheduler

`MeshJobQueue` became `MeshScheduler`: same stale-result rule, now with ordering
and bounds. It is a scheduler for derived geometry, not a general task framework.

### Classes first, distance second

Distance alone is the wrong metric — a distant section the player is looking at
matters more than a near one behind them. The **host** decides *why* a section is
wanted, because only it can see; the scheduler orders within that.

```text
1  RequiredDirty     visible geometry that is now wrong
2  RequiredMissing   visible geometry that does not exist yet
3  DirtyOutOfView    resident but stale, not currently looked at
4  PrefetchNear      speculative, close
5  PrefetchDistant   speculative, far
```

Within a class: nearer, then older, then section id. The last tie-break exists so
equal-priority work dispatches identically on every run — thread completion order
must never decide what the scheduler does next.

### Starvation

A request's class is promoted one step per `starvation_ticks` waited, so anything
queued long enough reaches the top class and is served. Age is counted in
scheduler ticks, never wall-clock. Re-requesting a pending section sharpens its
urgency but **does not reset its age**, so a section invalidated every tick cannot
starve itself.

### Bounds

`max_active_mesh_jobs` (snapshot memory and worker load) · 
`max_results_applied_per_tick` (frame spike from uploads) · 
`max_pending_mesh_requests` (reported when exceeded, not silently absorbed).

Completed results land in an inbox and are drained up to the per-tick limit.
Stale results are discarded without consuming that budget, because discarding
costs nothing and must not crowd out real work.

### A conservatism the tests exposed

A fingerprint records neighbour **whole-volume revisions**, so an edit deep inside
a neighbour invalidates a section even though it could not have changed the shared
boundary. Two tests initially failed on this and the tests were wrong, not the
code: over-invalidating costs a recompile, under-invalidating leaves a wall where
the player carved a hole. Now documented and asserted directly. A boundary-shell
revision would be tighter and is a later optimisation if measurement justifies it.

### Verified

Twenty-two scheduler tests: near before far, classes dominating distance, every
class ordering as declared, stable ties, deduplication, urgency sharpening without
downgrade, age surviving re-requests, ageing overtaking newer nearer work,
promotion capped at the top class, dispatch bounded by active jobs, **a queued
request owning no cell data**, snapshots taken at dispatch, apply limit bounding a
burst, stale results not consuming apply budget, requeue on staleness, pending
capacity bound, prefetch cancellation for teleports, and dispatch order
reproducible across runs.

227 tests pass.

## 0002.9 (engine side) — camera-relative streaming

`RegionStreamer` decides what should be resident and emits `StreamAction`s; the
host performs them on whatever threads it likes and reports back. No I/O, no
threads, no Bevy — which is why every ordering below is reachable in a unit test.

Three asynchronous activities are tracked **separately**, because they have
different staleness rules and different failure modes: region loads, region saves,
mesh compiles.

### Staleness is not only a mesh problem

**Save staleness is the rule that most easily eats a player's work.**

```text
region at revision 40
  → save snapshots revision 40
  → worker starts writing
  → player edits; region becomes revision 41
  → save of revision 40 completes
```

Marking it clean there discards edit 41 at the next eviction. A `SaveTicket`
records the revision it captured, and completion marks the region clean only if
that is still the revision in memory. Otherwise it stays dirty and is written
again before it can be evicted.

**Load staleness** matters after a teleport, and worse, an in-flight load started
*before* a save could carry pre-save cells back over newer ones. Each request
carries a per-region generation; a result is installed only if it is still the
current request and the camera still wants it.

### Two bugs the tests caught

**A region left in `Saving` was never dropped.** `update` skipped anything
`is_in_flight()`, and `finish_save` deliberately leaves the state as `Saving` so
the drop is explicit — so the region lingered resident forever. Now a droppable
region is checked before any state test, and "in flight" means the host still owes
a result, not merely that the state says so.

**A missing shard was treated as a load failure**, so unexplored regions retried
forever. `RegionLoad` now distinguishes `Absent` (no shard: normal, empty terrain,
becomes resident and empty) from `Failed` (exists but unreadable: retried, and
never silently turned into empty terrain). Conflating those either spins forever
or destroys a world.

### Verified

Eighteen streamer tests: loading ahead and evicting behind, hysteresis preventing
churn across the boundary, edit-during-save staying dirty, clean saves marking
clean, failed saves keeping the region resident and dirty, **edit → save → evict →
travel → return preserving the edit**, loads discarded after the camera leaves,
a superseded load unable to overwrite a newer one, failed loads retried, absent
shards accepted as empty, teleport converging without loading the route, rapid
direction changes not corrupting residency, bounded loads, nearest-first ordering,
eviction cancelled by renewed interest, and a dirty region never dropped unsaved.

245 tests pass; stress baseline unchanged.

## 0002.9 (host) — it streams

The sandbox now writes the demo world out as a v2 directory on first run and
**starts empty**, with regions arriving around the camera. Region loads, region
saves and mesh compiles all run on Bevy's async compute pool; no ordinary path
compiles geometry on the main schedule any more.

Three pieces of host wiring the engine could not do for itself:

- **Task spawning.** `drive_streaming` turns `StreamAction`s into pool tasks and
  `poll_region_tasks` / `apply_mesh_results` report results back. A compiled
  result is applied only if the scheduler still judges it current.
- **Renderer asset lifetime.** Evicting world data is not enough: when a region
  drops, its cache entries, Bevy mesh assets and entities go with it. Dropping the
  entity releases the last strong handle to its mesh, which is what stops travel
  leaving a trail of dead meshes.
- **Picking moved into the engine.** `engine_world::raycast`, with fourteen tests
  covering what the sandbox version never had.

### Measured in the running app

Startup, streaming `saves/world`: `wanted 125  resident 125`, `applied 22  stale 2`
— asynchronous compilation running, and two in-flight results correctly discarded
as regions arrived and invalidated their neighbours.

Flying continuously for about 90 seconds across roughly a thousand cells:

| | |
|---|---|
| camera | `-791 -508 -929`, region `-7 -4 -8` |
| render origin | `-768 -512 -1024` — every component region-aligned |
| residency | **270 → 266 → 270** across three further flights |
| entities / mesh bytes | 0 / 0 B once past the demo world |
| panics | none |

**Residency is bounded.** It settles around 268 rather than growing, which is the
unload-radius working set — deliberately larger than the 125 wanted, because
hysteresis is what stops regions churning at the boundary. The entity and mesh-byte
counts dropping to zero is the renderer teardown working: nothing was leaked
behind the camera.

The compiler toggle became a `Copy` enum rather than a trait object, because the
choice has to travel with a job to a worker thread.

## 0002.10 — memory budget and eviction

Residency was bounded by *distance* alone. The very first baseline showed why that
is unsafe: mesh bytes per cell range from **0.1 B** on solid terrain to **1,296 B**
on the checker case — four orders of magnitude. A region count is therefore not a
memory bound at all, and any budget denominated in regions is wrong by that factor.

So the budget is denominated in bytes, and the bytes are accounted where they
actually live:

| `MemoryAccount` field | owner |
|---|---|
| `volume_bytes` | world cell storage |
| `palette_bytes` | world palettes |
| `cpu_mesh_bytes` | the section mesh cache |
| `mesh_snapshot_bytes` | cells copied into compiling jobs |
| `awaiting_apply_bytes` | results returned and not yet uploaded |
| `io_snapshot_bytes` | regions copied for a save in flight |

`resident_bytes()` and `in_flight_bytes()` are kept apart deliberately: **evicting
regions does nothing about work already in flight**, so a budget that mixed them
would respond to in-flight pressure by throwing away the wrong thing.

Only the host can add these up — the world, the cache and the scheduler each own a
piece — so the host measures and the engine is *told*
(`RegionStreamer::note_memory`). The engine never reaches into the renderer.

### Distance prefers; the budget decides

`MemoryBudget` has a soft target and a hard ceiling, and `Pressure` is the one
value the policy reads:

| pressure | load radius | unload radius | prefetch |
|---|---|---|---|
| `Comfortable` | as configured | as configured | yes |
| `OverSoft` | configured − 1 | configured − 1 | held back |
| `OverHard` | 0 | shrunk hard | held back |

Reaching *outward* stops before anything already resident is thrown away — under
mild pressure the engine stops being greedy, and only real pressure makes it give
ground. Two counters make this visible rather than mysterious:
`loads_withheld` and `evicted_for_budget`. A radius that silently shrank is
exposed as `load_radius_in_use()` and shown on the HUD.

### In-flight backpressure (0002.10c)

The job count was the only bound on snapshot memory, and it is a bad one for the
same reason region counts are: a snapshot of a dense section costs orders of
magnitude more than one of empty air, so "four jobs" can mean a few kilobytes or
many megabytes. `SchedulerLimits::max_in_flight_snapshot_bytes` bounds it
directly.

Three details that are easy to get wrong, each with a test:

- **One job always goes through.** A section whose snapshot exceeds the whole
  ceiling must still be meshed, or that part of the world would have a permanent
  hole in it. The check is skipped when nothing is in flight.
- **The queue stops, it does not skip.** Dispatch breaks at the first job that
  will not fit rather than looking past it for a cheaper one, because skipping
  would let a distant empty section jump ahead of a near solid one — priority
  inversion bought with a few bytes.
- **Every exit path releases the bytes.** Applied, stale, unwanted, cancelled:
  `active` is now a map of section → snapshot bytes, and all four paths remove
  from it. Counting on dispatch and forgetting one release path would leak the
  budget until nothing could ever be dispatched again.

The ceiling cannot be known before snapshotting (the cost *is* the snapshot), so
the job is built, measured, and only then accepted or put back.

### What it measured

The stress baseline is **byte-identical** — 0002.10 adds accounting and policy,
and changes no geometry. The sandbox runs with a deliberately small 96 MiB
ceiling (72 MiB soft): a budget nobody ever reaches is a budget that was never
tested.

## 0002.11 — renderer granularity audit

`SectionGrid` has been one volume per render section since DROP 0001. That was the
*measurement baseline*, not a decision, and DROP 0002's premise is that an
unmeasured default is a guess. So it was measured, at 1, 2 and 4 volumes per
section edge, across all seven scenarios:

```
cargo run --release -p engine_stress --bin granularity
```

**Verdict: keep `SectionGrid = 1`.** The one real win needs hardware this
environment does not have, and the measured costs are immediate.

### What a single default has to serve

| grid | volumes/section | sections | total mesh | largest section | bytes/edit | uploads/edit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | 1 | **309** | 25.3 MiB | **2.5 MiB** | **68.8 KiB** | 1.47 |
| 2 | 8 | 44 | 25.3 MiB | 20.2 MiB | 80.7 KiB | **1.00** |
| 4 | 64 | **17** | 25.3 MiB | 20.2 MiB | 80.7 KiB | **1.00** |

### The three findings

**Total mesh memory does not change at all** — 25.3 MiB at every grid, to the
byte. That is the design working as intended: sections group quads by
*concatenation* and never merge across a volume boundary, so coarsening cannot
buy geometry. It also means granularity is not a lever on the memory problem,
which is what 0002.12 is for.

**Coarsening buys draw calls, and only draw calls.** 309 → 17 render units is an
18× reduction in entities and draw calls. That is a genuine and potentially large
win — and it is a *GPU* win, which a software rasteriser cannot measure. Per the
audit's own decision rule, it needs real-GPU evidence before it changes anything.

**Coarsening costs edit latency, now, measurably.** A section is one buffer, so a
single changed cell recompiles and re-uploads the whole thing:

| scenario | grid 1 | grid 2 | grid 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| `edit_storm` median rebuild | **0.99 ms** | 8.84 ms | 13.49 ms |
| `boundary_storm` median rebuild | **2.12 ms** | 7.73 ms | 12.59 ms |

13.5 ms for one cell is a dropped frame at 60 Hz. The largest single buffer also
grows 8× (2.5 → 20.2 MiB), so one edit's re-upload becomes a frame spike, and job
snapshots grow with the section too (`dense_solid`: 42.3 KiB → 512.2 KiB per job,
12×).

### The honest counter-argument

Coarsening is not all cost. Neighbours that were separate sections are inside one,
so an edit dirties fewer render units and invalidates fewer in-flight jobs:

| | grid 1 | grid 2 | grid 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| uploads per edit | 1.47 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| fingerprint invalidations per edit | 4.00 | **1.00** | **1.00** |

Four stale-job invalidations per edit down to one is a real improvement to async
behaviour. It is simply much smaller than a 13× latency regression.

### What makes this a decision and not a guess

Granularity stays **configurable and uncoupled**, and that is now asserted rather
than asserted-in-prose. `crates/engine_stress/tests/granularity.rs` proves at
every audited grid that:

- the surface is **bit-identical** — same quads, vertices, indices and bytes. If a
  coarser grid ever produced fewer quads it would mean the mesher had begun merging
  across volumes, which would let an edit in one volume change geometry in another
  and make incremental rebuilds unsound.
- coarsening never *increases* section count, and never *decreases* the largest
  buffer or a job snapshot — the trade runs the direction the audit claims.
- the shipped default is still the finest audited grid, so coarsening it later
  fails a test that names the audit, instead of slipping through as a tweak.

So the knob can be turned on real-GPU evidence later without reopening the mesher.
That was the point.

### One bottleneck the audit exposed

`SectionMeshCache::rebuild_sections` compiles *every* volume a section covers,
including volumes that do not exist. At grid 1 that is free — a dirty section is
exactly one populated volume. At grid 4 on an 8-volume world it means scanning 56
empty volumes per edit, which is most of that 13.5 ms. It is only worth fixing if
granularity is ever coarsened, so it is recorded here rather than pre-emptively
optimised. Noted for DROP 0003.
